下面讲 SN1 和 SN2。两者都是亲核取代（nucleophilic substitution）：亲核试剂 Nu⁻ 取代碳上的离去基团 LG。名字里的 S 指 substitution（取代），N 指 nucleophilic（亲核），数字 1 或 2 指决速步里有几个分子参与。

SN2：一步完成（协同机理，concerted）

  • Nu⁻ 从离去基团的正背面（180°）进攻碳，旧键断裂和新键形成同时发生。整个过程只有一个过渡态，没有中间体。
  • 速率 = k[底物][Nu]。两个分子都出现在唯一的决速步里，所以是二级反应。
  • 立体化学：构型翻转（Walden inversion）。例如 (R)-2-溴丁烷与 CN⁻ 反应，得到 (S) 产物。
  • 底物活性：甲基 > 1° > 2° ≫ 3°，3° 基本不反应。原因是背面进攻需要空间，烷基越多，位阻越大。
  • 有利条件：强亲核试剂（常为负离子，如 CN⁻、I⁻、RS⁻、N₃⁻），以及极性非质子溶剂（DMSO、DMF、丙酮）。非质子溶剂不会用氢键包住负离子，所以 Nu⁻ 保持"裸露"，活性更高。

SN1：两步完成

  1. 离去基团先离开，生成碳正离子。这一步慢，是决速步。
  2. Nu 进攻碳正离子。这一步快。
  • 速率 = k[底物]。决速步只涉及底物，所以 Nu 的浓度和强弱不影响速率。
  • 立体化学：碳正离子是 sp² 平面结构，Nu 可以从两面进攻，所以产物外消旋（racemization）。实际上翻转产物常常略多，因为离去基团刚离开时还挡着一面（离子对效应，ion pair）。
  • 底物活性：3° > 2°，烯丙位和苄位也很好；甲基和 1° 基本不走 SN1。决定因素是碳正离子的稳定性：超共轭、诱导效应、共振越多，越稳定。
  • 有利条件：弱亲核试剂即可（H₂O、ROH，溶剂本身就是亲核试剂，这类反应叫溶剂解 solvolysis），以及极性质子溶剂。质子溶剂能稳定碳正离子和离去的负离子，从而降低第一步的能垒。
  • 特有现象：碳正离子可能发生重排（1,2-氢迁移或甲基迁移），变成更稳定的碳正离子。另外常与 E1 消除竞争。

两者的共同要求：好的离去基团
离去能力为 I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ ≫ F⁻，TsO⁻ 也很好。判断标准是 LG⁻ 越弱碱（即共轭酸越强）越好离开。OH⁻ 是差的离去基团，需要先质子化成 H₂O 才能离开。

判断顺序

  1. 先看底物：甲基或 1° 走 SN2；3° 走 SN1（若用强碱，则会走消除）。
  2. 2° 底物是模糊区，要看试剂和溶剂：强 Nu 加非质子溶剂走 SN2；弱 Nu 加质子溶剂走 SN1。
  3. 立体化学和速率规律都由机理推出，只要记住"一步还是两步、有没有碳正离子"，其余结论都能推回来。

Explain SN1 and SN2

Claude

Both are nucleophilic substitution reactions: a nucleophile (Nu⁻) replaces a leaving group (LG) on a carbon. The number in each name tells you how many molecules take part in the rate-determining step.

SN2: one step (concerted)

  • The nucleophile attacks the carbon from the back side, 180° opposite the leaving group. The new bond forms while the old bond breaks, so there is one transition state and no intermediate.
  • Rate = k[substrate][Nu]. Both species are in the only step, which makes it second order.
  • Stereochemistry: inversion of configuration (Walden inversion). For example, (R)-2-bromobutane reacts with CN⁻ to give the (S) product.
  • Substrate reactivity: methyl > 1° > 2° ≫ 3°, and 3° essentially doesn't react. Backside attack needs room, and each extra alkyl group blocks it (steric hindrance).
  • Favored by:
    • Strong nucleophiles, which are often anions such as CN⁻, I⁻, RS⁻ and N₃⁻.
    • Polar aprotic solvents such as DMSO, DMF and acetone. These don't hydrogen-bond to the anion, so the nucleophile stays "naked" and more reactive.

SN1: two steps

  1. The leaving group departs and forms a carbocation. This step is slow and rate-determining.
  2. The nucleophile attacks the carbocation. This step is fast.
  • Rate = k[substrate]. Only the substrate is in the slow step, so the nucleophile's concentration and strength don't affect the rate.
  • Stereochemistry: the carbocation is planar (sp²), so attack can occur from either face, giving racemization. In practice you often get slightly more inversion, because the departing leaving group briefly shields one face (the ion-pair effect).
  • Substrate reactivity: 3° > 2°, and allylic and benzylic substrates are also good. Methyl and 1° substrates don't go SN1 because their carbocations are too unstable. Carbocation stability, from hyperconjugation, induction and resonance, is what matters.
  • Favored by:
    • Weak nucleophiles, such as H₂O or ROH. When the solvent itself acts as the nucleophile, the reaction is called solvolysis.
    • Polar protic solvents. They stabilize the carbocation and the departing anion, which lowers the barrier of step 1.
  • Watch for:
    • Rearrangements: 1,2-hydride or 1,2-methyl shifts that give a more stable carbocation.
    • Competition with E1 elimination.

Both need a good leaving group
Leaving-group ability goes I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ ≫ F⁻, and TsO⁻ is excellent. The weaker the base LG⁻ is (the stronger its conjugate acid), the better it leaves. OH⁻ is a poor leaving group until it is protonated to H₂O.

How to decide

  1. Look at the substrate first. Methyl and 1° substrates go SN2. 3° substrates go SN1, or elimination if a strong base is present.
  2. 2° substrates are the gray zone, so look at the nucleophile and solvent. A strong nucleophile in an aprotic solvent points to SN2; a weak nucleophile in a protic solvent points to SN1.
  3. Everything else follows from the mechanism. If you know whether it is one step or two, and whether a carbocation forms, you can derive the rate law and the stereochemistry.

3. 讲法：同一个知识点的三种讲法
下面都用同一个问题示范：SN2 反应为什么会让碳的构型反转？

讲法 A｜直接给结论、条件和原因
SN2 是一步完成的反应：亲核试剂（nucleophile）从离去基团（leaving group）的正对面进攻碳，新键形成和旧键断裂同时发生。结果是碳上另外三个基团的空间排布反转，叫 Walden inversion。只有当这个碳是手性碳时，反转才能从产物上看出来。

讲法 B｜先摆实验现象，再推出机理
实验观察：(R)-2-溴丁烷和 OH⁻ 反应，产物几乎只有 (S)-2-丁醇，而不是 R、S 各一半。
从这个现象能推出两件事：

  • 产物只有一种构型，说明反应过程中没有生成一个平面中间体再被两侧随机进攻。
  • 构型正好相反，说明 OH⁻ 是从 Br 的正对面进来的。

推完之后，才给出 SN2 这个名字和它的机理。

讲法 C｜从轨道讲到底
亲核试剂的孤对电子要成键，必须进入一个空轨道。C–Br 键上能接收电子的低能空轨道是反键轨道 σ*，它的主要部分位于碳原子背离 Br 的那一侧。所以亲核试剂只能从背面进攻。
电子进入 σ* 后，C–Br 键被削弱直到断开。与此同时，碳上的三个基团先被推到同一个平面（过渡态），再翻到另一侧，于是构型反转。
同一个原因还能解释另一件事：叔碳几乎不发生 SN2，因为它的背面被三个基团挡住了。

  • A 最快拿到结论。
  • B 让你看到结论是怎么从证据里推出来的。
  • C 追到最底层的原因，并且能顺带解释别的现象，篇幅最长。

你可以选一种、组合使用（比如"先 B 引入，再用 C 讲透"），也可以让我按内容难度自己决定。

We'll start with Concept 1: the overview of nucleophilic substitution: what the reaction does, and the three roles in it (substrate, nucleophile, leaving group). Every later concept is a refinement of the logic set up here.

What the reaction is

In a nucleophilic substitution, an electron-pair donor forms a new bond to an sp³ carbon. At the same time or earlier, a group already attached to that carbon departs and takes the electron pair of its old bond with it.

General equation:

Nu:⁻ + R–X → R–Nu + X:⁻

  • Nu is the nucleophile, the species that brings the electron pair.
  • ":" is a lone pair (two non-bonding electrons) on the nucleophile's attacking atom.
  • "⁻" is a negative formal charge. Many nucleophiles are anions, but not all. Neutral ones come up below.
  • R is the carbon framework (an alkyl group) attached to the reacting carbon.
  • X is the leaving group. In this unit it is most often Cl, Br, or I.
  • R–X is the substrate, the molecule being changed.
  • X:⁻ is the leaving group after it departs. It now holds the two electrons that used to be the C–X bond.

The net bond change is that one σ bond forms (C–Nu) and one σ bond breaks (C–X). The reacting carbon has four bonds before and four after. Only one of its partners is swapped, and that swap is what "substitution" means.

Here is a concrete case: HO⁻ + CH₃CH₂–Br → CH₃CH₂–OH + Br⁻.

  • HO⁻ is hydroxide, the nucleophile.
  • CH₃CH₂–Br is bromoethane, the substrate.
  • Br⁻ is bromide, the leaving group.

In the lab, HO⁻ is added as NaOH. Na⁺ does not take part (it is a spectator ion), so it is left out of mechanisms.

Curved-arrow rules used in the diagrams:

  • A double-barbed curved arrow shows the movement of two electrons.
  • The tail starts where the electrons are now: a lone pair, or the middle of a bond.
  • The head ends where they go. That is either an atom, where they become a lone pair, or the space between two atoms, where they become a new bond.

The substrate

The substrate is the molecule that gets changed. For SN1/SN2 it is usually an alkyl halide, meaning a halogen bonded to an sp³ carbon. Two positions on it have names:

  • The α-carbon (α is Greek "alpha", meaning "first position") is the carbon directly bonded to X, the leaving group. Substitution happens here.
  • A β-carbon (β is "beta", second position) is any carbon bonded to the α-carbon. Hydrogens on β-carbons play no part in substitution. A base can remove one of them in elimination, though (Concept 14), which is why the same reagents can give two different products.

Why the nucleophile goes to the α-carbon. There are two reasons, at two depths.

The first reason is bond polarity.

  • The Pauling electronegativities are C 2.55, Cl 3.16, Br 2.96, and I 2.66.
  • In C–Cl and C–Br, the bonding electrons are pulled toward X.
  • The α-carbon therefore carries a partial positive charge, written δ+. δ is Greek "delta", and δ+ means a positive charge smaller than +1.
  • X carries the matching δ−.
  • An electron-pair donor is attracted to the δ+ site.

The second reason is about orbitals, which is the deeper layer.

  • The lowest-energy empty orbital of R–X (R is the alkyl framework) is called its LUMO, the "lowest unoccupied molecular orbital".
  • That LUMO is the C–X σ* orbital ("sigma-star"), the antibonding partner of the C–X σ bond.
  • σ* has its largest lobe on carbon, on the side pointing away from X.
  • The nucleophile's lone pair sits in its HOMO, the "highest occupied molecular orbital".
  • A new bond forms when HOMO electrons flow into the LUMO.
  • Electrons placed in σ* cancel the C–X bonding interaction, so filling σ* is exactly what breaks the C–X bond.

So forming C–Nu (Nu is the nucleophile) and breaking C–X are linked through this one orbital. Concept 3 uses it to explain which side the nucleophile must approach from.

The polarity argument has a limit. By electronegativity, C–I is almost nonpolar (2.55 vs 2.66), yet alkyl iodides are the most reactive alkyl halides in substitution. So polarity is not what controls reactivity. Two other things do:

  • How weak the C–X bond is. Approximate bond dissociation energies for CH₃–X are C–Cl ≈ 350, C–Br ≈ 295, and C–I ≈ 235 kJ/mol.
  • How stable X⁻ is once it has left.

Both favor iodide. Concept 6 develops the second point.

Degree of the substrate. Count the carbons bonded to the α-carbon:

  • Methyl, CH₃–X, has zero.
  • Primary, written 1°, has one. Example: CH₃CH₂–Br.
  • Secondary, written 2°, has two. Example: (CH₃)₂CH–Br.
  • Tertiary, written 3°, has three. Example: (CH₃)₃C–Br.

This number is the most important structural variable in the unit, and it pushes the two mechanisms in opposite directions:

  • SN2 is fastest for methyl and 1° and essentially absent at 3°, because the α-carbon gets crowded (Concept 4).
  • SN1 is fastest at 3° and essentially absent for methyl and 1°, because of carbocation stability (Concept 9).

The reasons come in those concepts. For now, just note that degree is the variable.

Condition: the α-carbon must be sp³. Suppose X sits on an sp² carbon instead. That is either a vinyl halide (X on a C=C carbon) or an aryl halide (X directly on a benzene ring). Then neither SN1 nor SN2 occurs under normal conditions, for three reasons:

  • An sp² orbital has 33% s character, compared with 25% for sp³. s electrons are held closer to the nucleus, so the sp² C–X bond is shorter and stronger.
  • In an aryl halide, the ring itself occupies the space opposite X, so the nucleophile cannot reach the back lobe of σ*.
  • The cation that would form if X⁻ left (a vinyl or aryl cation) is very unstable (Concept 9).

Allylic and benzylic halides are the contrast case. There X is on an sp³ carbon next to a C=C or a ring, and these are unusually reactive. The only difference from vinyl and aryl halides is which carbon X is attached to.

The nucleophile

A nucleophile is a species that donates an electron pair to an electron-poor atom to form a new covalent bond. The name means "nucleus-loving": it is attracted to positive charge.

Every nucleophile is a Lewis base, meaning an electron-pair donor. The two words describe the same electron pair aimed at different targets: "nucleophile" when it attacks carbon, "base" when it attacks H⁺. They are not the same property, though:

  • Nucleophilicity is how fast a species attacks carbon. It is a kinetic property.
  • Basicity is how strongly a species holds H⁺ at equilibrium. It is a thermodynamic property, measured by the pKa of the conjugate acid.

The two often run together but can diverge (Concept 5).

Nucleophiles come in two kinds:

  • Anionic: HO⁻ (hydroxide), CH₃O⁻ (methoxide), CN⁻ (cyanide), HS⁻ (hydrosulfide), N₃⁻ (azide), I⁻ (iodide), CH₃COO⁻ (acetate).
  • Neutral: H₂O, CH₃OH, NH₃, and amines such as (CH₃)₃N.

A neutral nucleophile gives a positively charged first product. The attacking atom keeps all its existing bonds and turns one of its lone pairs into a new bond. Example:

H₂O + (CH₃)₃C–Br → (CH₃)₃C–OH₂⁺ + Br⁻

The formal charge (FC) on that oxygen is:

FC = (valence electrons of the free atom) − (non-bonding electrons) − ½(bonding electrons) = 6 − 2 − ½(6) = +1

  • The first 6 is oxygen's six valence electrons.
  • The 2 is its one remaining lone pair.
  • The second 6 is the electrons in its three bonds (two O–H, one O–C).

The cation then loses H⁺ to a second water molecule in an ordinary acid–base step, giving (CH₃)₃C–OH plus H₃O⁺. When the solvent itself is the nucleophile, as here, the reaction is called a solvolysis. The term returns in Concept 8.

The leaving group

The leaving group is the atom or group that departs from the α-carbon carrying both electrons of the old C–X bond. A bond split so that both electrons go to one atom is called heterolytic cleavage. Homolytic cleavage, with one electron going to each atom, gives radicals and is not part of this unit.

The requirement for a good leaving group follows from what it has to do. After leaving, it must be stable holding that electron pair on its own. How content an anion is to hold its electron pair without bonding to H⁺ is what basicity measures, in reverse. So a weak base is a good leaving group.

The measure is the pKa of the leaving group's conjugate acid:

  • pKa = −log₁₀ Ka, where Ka is the acid dissociation constant of H–X.
  • A low or negative pKa means H–X gives up H⁺ easily.
  • That makes X⁻ a weak base, and therefore a good leaving group.

Here is each leaving group, its conjugate acid, the approximate pKa, and the verdict:

  • I⁻: conjugate acid HI, pKa ≈ −10. Excellent.
  • Br⁻: conjugate acid HBr, pKa ≈ −9. Excellent.
  • Cl⁻: conjugate acid HCl, pKa ≈ −7. Good.
  • H₂O, when it leaves from R–OH₂⁺: conjugate acid H₃O⁺, pKa ≈ −1.7. Good.
  • HO⁻: conjugate acid H₂O, pKa ≈ 15.7. Very poor.
  • CH₃O⁻: conjugate acid CH₃OH, pKa ≈ 15.5. Very poor.

The last two explain a fact you will use constantly. Alcohols (R–OH) and ethers (R–O–R) do not undergo SN1/SN2 directly, because the group that would leave is a strong base. Converting –OH into a good leaving group is Concept 15.

The pKa link is a strong correlation, not a law. Fluoride is the main case where it needs refining (Concept 6).

Which direction is downhill

The rule: when the nucleophile and the leaving group are both anions, the substitution is thermodynamically favorable if the leaving group is a weaker base than the nucleophile.

Why it holds:

  • Basicity measures how strongly an anion bonds to H⁺.
  • To a first approximation, it also tracks how strongly the anion bonds to carbon.
  • A stronger base forms a stronger bond to carbon and is less stable as a free ion.
  • So the equilibrium favors the side where the stronger base is bonded to carbon and the weaker base is free.

This is the same logic by which acid–base equilibria favor the weaker acid and the weaker base.

Example: HO⁻ + CH₃CH₂–Br → CH₃CH₂–OH + Br⁻

  • The nucleophile is HO⁻. Its conjugate acid is H₂O, pKa ≈ 15.7.
  • The leaving group is Br⁻. Its conjugate acid is HBr, pKa ≈ −9.
  • Br⁻ is by far the weaker base, so the forward reaction is favorable.
  • The reverse, Br⁻ + CH₃CH₂–OH, would have to release HO⁻, so it does not occur.

The rule holds only under these conditions:

  1. The charge type is the same on both sides (an anion goes in, an anion comes out). When a neutral nucleophile produces a cation, charges and solvation change, and the simple base comparison is no longer enough.
  2. It predicts where the equilibrium lies, not how fast the reaction goes. A favorable reaction can still have no usable pathway.
  3. The equilibrium can be pushed by removing a product.

The Finkelstein reaction shows the third condition: R–Cl + NaI, in acetone, gives R–I + NaCl.

  • Cl⁻ (HCl, pKa ≈ −7) is a stronger base than I⁻ (HI, pKa ≈ −10).
  • By the rule, the forward direction is therefore not favored in solution on its own.
  • It works anyway because NaI dissolves in acetone and NaCl does not.
  • NaCl precipitates, which removes Cl⁻ from solution, and Le Chatelier's principle pulls the reaction forward.

Connection to physical chemistry. "Favorable" means ΔG° < 0, where ΔG° = −RT ln K.

  • ΔG° is the standard Gibbs free energy change (products minus reactants).
  • R is the gas constant, 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. This R is unrelated to the alkyl group R.
  • T is the temperature in kelvin.
  • K is the equilibrium constant.
  • ΔG° < 0 is equivalent to K > 1.

How fast a reaction goes is set by a different quantity, the activation energy Ea, which is the height of the energy barrier between reactants and products. ΔG° depends only on the starting and ending states.

So whenever SN1 and SN2 would turn the same reactants into the identical product, they have the same ΔG°. They differ only in path, barrier height, and rate law. Choosing between SN1 and SN2 is therefore entirely a kinetic question. That is why Concepts 2, 8 and 12 are about rate laws, rate-determining steps, and energy diagrams.

The central question: timing

Overall, one bond to the α-carbon forms and one breaks. The question that splits this unit into SN1 and SN2 is the order in which this happens. There are only three possible orders.

What decides among them is a hard limit on carbon. Carbon's valence shell has four orbitals (one 2s and three 2p), so it can hold at most four electron pairs, which is the octet.

The second diagram lays out the three options.

Why each row ends where it does:

  • Concerted (row 1). There is only one step, and the nucleophile and substrate are both in it. This is SN2.
  • Leaving group first (row 2). The intermediate is a carbocation: a carbon with three bonds, six valence electrons and a +1 charge. It is sp² with an empty p orbital. Carbon can exist this way because it is below the octet, not above it. The nucleophile then fills the empty p orbital in a second step. This is SN1.
  • Nucleophile first (row 3). Carbon would need five full bonds, which is ten valence electrons. The n = 2 shell has only s and p orbitals; there is no 2d orbital. So carbon has no fifth valence orbital, and this cannot be a real intermediate.

One clarification now, to prevent confusion later. The SN2 transition state does show both Nu and X attached to carbon at the same moment. Those are two partial bonds at an energy maximum, which cannot be isolated. It is not an intermediate with five full bonds. Concepts 2 and 3 make this precise.

The names:

  • S stands for substitution.
  • N stands for nucleophilic.
  • The number is the molecularity of the rate-determining step. Molecularity is the number of species that come together in that step. The rate-determining step is the slowest step, and the overall rate cannot exceed its rate.
  • 2 means bimolecular: substrate plus nucleophile.
  • 1 means unimolecular: the substrate alone.

The number is not the count of steps. SN1 actually has more steps than SN2.

Since you know reaction order from kinetics, here are the rate laws as a preview:

  • SN2 is second order overall: rate = k[R–X][Nu⁻].
  • SN1 is first order: rate = k[R–X].
  • In both, k is the rate constant and square brackets mean molar concentration.

Concepts 2 and 8 explain why each mechanism produces its rate law.

Problem 1. CH₃S⁻Na⁺ + (CH₃)₂CH–Br → (CH₃)₂CH–S–CH₃ + Na⁺Br⁻

(a) Identify the substrate, the nucleophile and its attacking atom, the leaving group, and the spectator ion.
(b) Identify the α-carbon and the β-carbons, and classify the substrate as methyl, 1°, 2°, or 3°.
(c) Describe the two curved arrows.
(d) Is the forward reaction thermodynamically favored? The pKa of CH₃SH is ≈ 10.4 and the pKa of HBr is ≈ −9.

Answer 1.

(a) Roles:

  • The substrate is (CH₃)₂CH–Br (2-bromopropane).
  • The nucleophile is CH₃S⁻ (methanethiolate), and it attacks through sulfur.
  • Sulfur carries the negative charge: FC = 6 − 6 − ½(2) = −1. That is 6 valence electrons, 6 non-bonding electrons in three lone pairs, and 2 bonding electrons in its one bond to carbon.
  • The leaving group is Br⁻.
  • Na⁺ is the spectator ion.

(b) Positions and degree:

  • The α-carbon is the central carbon, C2, which bears Br.
  • The β-carbons are the two CH₃ carbons, C1 and C3, which carry six β-hydrogens in total.
  • The α-carbon is bonded to two carbons, so the substrate is 2°.

(c) Arrows:

  • First arrow: tail on a lone pair on S, head at the α-carbon. This forms the C–S bond.
  • Second arrow: tail at the middle of the C–Br bond, head on Br. This turns the bonding pair into a lone pair on bromide.

(d) Direction:

  • Both the nucleophile and the leaving group are anions, so the base rule applies cleanly.
  • The nucleophile's conjugate acid has pKa ≈ 10.4. The leaving group's conjugate acid has pKa ≈ −9.
  • Br⁻ is the much weaker base, so the reaction is favored.

What this problem does not settle: whether the reaction goes by SN1 or SN2, and whether elimination competes at a 2° carbon. Nothing in Concept 1 decides those. It is a question of kinetics, and this substrate will come back later.

Problem 2. For each reaction, say whether it proceeds as written under ordinary SN1/SN2 conditions, and give the deciding reason.

(a) CH₃CH₂CH₂–OH + NaBr → CH₃CH₂CH₂–Br + NaOH
(b) CH₃–Cl + NaOCH₃ → CH₃–O–CH₃ + NaCl
(c) C₆H₅–Br (bromobenzene) + NaOH → C₆H₅–OH + NaBr

Answer 2.

(a) No.

  • The nucleophile is Br⁻ (HBr, pKa ≈ −9).
  • The group that would have to leave is HO⁻ (H₂O, pKa ≈ 15.7).
  • That would replace a very weak base with a very strong one, so the equilibrium lies on the reactant side.
  • The –OH must first be converted into a better leaving group (Concept 15).

(b) Yes.

  • The nucleophile is CH₃O⁻ (CH₃OH, pKa ≈ 15.5).
  • The leaving group is Cl⁻ (HCl, pKa ≈ −7). The leaving group is the far weaker base.
  • The substrate is methyl on an sp³ carbon, so there is no structural obstacle.
  • This reaction is a Williamson ether synthesis (Concept 15).

(c) No.

  • The base comparison alone would point toward the products, since HO⁻ is a much stronger base than Br⁻.
  • But Br is on an sp² ring carbon, which violates the sp³ condition.
  • The sp² C–Br bond is stronger, the ring blocks backside approach, and the aryl cation is too unstable.

This is the key lesson of (c): a favorable ΔG° (Gibbs free energy change) is necessary but not sufficient. There also has to be an accessible pathway. Aryl halides can be substituted under forcing conditions (around 350 °C, for example) or when the ring carries strongly electron-withdrawing groups. Those reactions run by different mechanisms (benzyne, or nucleophilic aromatic substitution), which are outside this unit.
